- (3016) proposed pathway: CG-6, excreta; propose only, nothing built; rows are `validation.excreta.*` in data/library/validation.json. Evidence levels follow the convention in these flags because PLATFORM.md section 4 was outside this task's read list, so the keeper should confirm: 3 a measured study read at abstract or table level, 4 a review median, secondary relay, regulatory standard or survey estimate, 5 no source. Every transfer is one physical flow with `Δsource = −q`, `Δdestination = +q` (PLATFORM §7a). Today `eatMeal` sends the unabsorbed part of each pooled nutrient and of food water to a sink named `unabsorbed`, and unabsorbed energy to `waste`, but `absorbedFraction` returns 1 for energy and for water whenever gut function is 1, so a healthy body currently leaves no energy or water residue; nothing records faeces or urine as a store. (1) FAECAL DRY MASS: food to `faeces`, q = unabsorbed solids, kg dry; plus body to `faeces` for the endogenous part (bacteria, sloughed cells, secretions), routed through `bodyMassDelta` (the sole body-mass writer, 2623). Median 29 g per day (12 to 81; 28 high-income, 38 low-income) of 128 g wet (Rose 2015 review, level 4); UK adults 106 g wet (Cummings 1992, 220 adults, level 3). Bacteria are 25 to 54% of dry solids in the review's abstract as the search result showed it, so dry mass is not only unabsorbed food; no page read says how much is endogenous body mass. (2) FAECAL WATER: food water unabsorbed (the existing `unabsorbed` water transfer) plus body water to `faeces` through `physiology.addBodyWater` as mass:water, q = 0.75 × wet mass − unabsorbed food water, floored at 0 (water 75%, range 63 to 86, Rose, level 4; about 96 g per day at 128 g wet). At near-equal fibre the vegetarian stool held 78.9% water against 72.6% (Reddy via Rose, level 4), so the water share is not a fixed constant. No page gave gut secretion volumes. (3) FIBRE AND DIET: wet mass = 101.3 + 2.96 × fibre g per day (±1.13 on the slope; Rose Figure 2, level 4), correlation 0.84 over 26 groups (Cummings, level 3), vegan 225 g at 47 g fibre against omnivore 153 g at 23 g; dry mass barely moves (omnivore 27.1 g, vegetarian 27.9 g, Van Faassen via Rose), so the extra wet mass is retained water, not extra solid. Needs a fibre content on each food, which this task did not check. (4) FAECAL NITROGEN: food to `faeces`, q = unabsorbed N of each pooled protein, g N. 1.8 g per day (0.9 to 4.9; Rose, level 4); 1.38 g, 19% of a 7.16 g N intake (19 Korean women, 김주연 1987, level 3); N absorption about 75% at 44 g protein against about 85% at 85 g (8 women, 구재옥 1988, level 3), so the unabsorbed share falls as protein rises. Rose's faecal protein median 6.3 g over 1.8 g N implies a factor of 3.5, not the textbook 6.25, because the two medians come from different study sets (n = 7 and 18); no conversion factor from protein to N was sourced here. (5) URINE WATER: body water to `urine`, q = urine volume, kg: median 1.42 L (0.8 to 2.45; Rose Table 10, level 4); fluid intake explained 78% of its variation in one sample (Parker and Gallagher via Rose). Physiology's existing daily water ledger may already carry a urine term; it was not read, so check it before adding this one. (6) URINE NITROGEN: body protein pool to `urine`, q = f × absorbed N, g N, f = 0.854 ± 0.036 of N intake (김주연 1987, level 3) and 'about 80%' (Kipnis via Rose, level 4: the primary abstract gives no recovery figure); absolute 11 g N median (2 to 35; Rose Table 8, level 4) or 6.03 g (김주연 1987); the 35 g upper end is a range of study means across diets and the page summary did not show which study gave it, so treat the range as uncertain. Urea is 75.2% of urinary N (level 3), rising from 79% to 85% as protein doubled (level 3). Bingham and Cummings 1985: urine N varies 11 to 18% within a person against 14 to 26% for intake, so one day's urine N is a noisy reading of that day's intake. (7) FAECAL ENERGY: the existing food to `waste` transfer keeps its form but its fraction needs a source: 4.6% of gross energy lost on a control diet and 8.0% with 16 g per day refined cellulose (100 minus the 95.4% and 92.0% digestibilities, Slavin and Marlett 1980, 7 women, level 3; with fibre energy subtracted digestibility did not differ, 96.1 vs 95.6%). Median 132 kcal per day (49 to 347; Rose, level 4); the review's 4115 kcal per kg dry solids (29 g × 4.115 = 119 kcal) lets faecal energy be derived from the dry-mass transfer in (1) instead of carried separately. Miles 1988 (12 men, 37 g vs 16 g fibre) and Baer 1997 (17 subjects) report lower energy availability on higher fibre but their abstracts carry no faecal energy number. (8) HORSE (565 kg Finnhorse mares, 4-day total collection, Saastamoinen 2021, level 3, one breed): fresh faeces 14.4 to 19.3 kg per day (18.7 on hay), faecal dry matter 3.2 to 3.6 kg, about 19 to 22% dry (80% water); urine 10.9 to 14.3 L; faecal N 43 to 52 g; urinary N 85 (hay) to 123 g unacidified, 64 to 104 g acidified. Faecal dry matter was 39 to 48% of dry matter intake (0.44 on hay, computed), a usable transfer rule: feed to `faeces` at (1 − digestibility) × dry matter intake. Scottish standard: 24 L excreta and 58 g N per day with no body weight (level 4); a Dutch owner survey: 228 ± 134 g N per day at 542 kg (a calculation from reported feeding, level 4). Total N differs fourfold between sources (58, about 136, 228 g). WHAT THE SOURCES LEAVE SILENT: the endogenous share of faecal solids and N; gut secretion and reabsorption volumes; urinary energy (urea, creatinine) in kcal; skin and sweat N (김주연 1987 reports none); a protein-to-N factor; the faecal energy share on a mixed diet whose energy intake is stated; and any horse faeces-and-urine review figure with a stated body weight. In Saastamoinen's own tables faecal plus urinary N (136 g on hay unacidified, 115 acidified) exceeds the roughly 110 g N intake, so the authors computed no balance; do not close a horse N ledger against that paper. DUP-45 (whether the excreta stores belong to nutrition, physiology or Life) is not decided here.
